Part One: Before You Play

Start Here

What This Is

This is a solo journaling game and a history lesson in one. You play one person moving through real events. You roll dice to see how things go, and you write about what happens in a journal, from that person’s point of view. Think of it as a choose-your-own-adventure that you write yourself.

There is no game master, no second player, and nothing to memorize. Read, roll, write, repeat.

Who Is Eddy Wake

You are Eddy Wake. Does the “Eddy” stand for Edward or Edith? Only you will ever know.

You come from a long line of Wakes: a family of explorers, renowned to themselves. You turn up at real moments in history, the kind of person who might appear in the margins of the history books, if at all. History happened around people like Eddy. This is one of their stories.

What You Need

  • Something to write with.
  • A journal. Any notebook or loose paper will do.
  • A 20-sided die (a d20). Three is ideal. If you have only one, roll it two or three times and treat the rolls as one set. No die at all? Use the Random Number Table at the back.

Five encounters, one a day, about 50 minutes each.

How to Play

Your Tracker

Eddy has three axes:

  • Body is anything physical: strength, work, endurance.
  • Mind is thinking it through: noticing, planning, judging.
  • Heart is resolve and connection: holding on, and reaching other people.

Each axis has a column of wound boxes. The row you are on tells you how to roll.

Before you begin, fill in both Peak boxes on every axis. You start at Steady: tired, but capable.

A Turn

  1. Pick an interaction. Its title tells you which axis to roll and what it costs: The Last Mile (Body, 1 Turn).
  2. Roll the dice for that axis’s level.
  3. Find your number in the table. Do what it says. Then, in your journal, note the axis and your roll, and answer the question in two or three sentences.

The Rules That Matter

  • You have 5 turns in each encounter. Interactions marked 0 Turns are free. When your 5 turns are spent, go to the Encounter End.
  • Wound: fill the next empty box on that axis. When both boxes in a row are full, you drop to the next row down.
  • Heal: erase your most recent wound on that axis. If that opens a box in the row above yours, move back up to that row.
  • No second tries: if an interaction gave you a wound, you can’t go back to it unless the text says so.
  • You don’t have to do everything. Pick the interactions that fit the story you’re telling.

Writing in Your Journal

Start each entry with the date (each encounter gives it), where Eddy is, and one sentence about what just happened. Then answer the question from the table.

December 19, 1777. We arrived at dusk. The camp looks nothing like I expected.

Spelling doesn’t matter. Drawings count.

That’s the whole game. Anything else is explained when it comes up.

Part Two: The Adventure

Eddy Wake at Valley Forge

Introduction

Setting: December 1777 through June 1778. Valley Forge, Pennsylvania. A plateau above the Schuylkill River, twenty miles from Philadelphia, six miles from a British army that has taken the city and gone into comfortable winter quarters.

The Historical Moment: Washington’s army arrived at Valley Forge in December 1777 having lost Philadelphia, the largest city in the colonies, and fought two losing engagements at Brandywine and Germantown. They were not defeated. They were depleted, unpaid, and undersupplied.

Player: You are Eddy Wake. The army at Valley Forge was not the monolithic thing that paintings later made it: it was twelve thousand men from different colonies, different trades, different faiths, different backgrounds. Farmers and artisans. Immigrants. Free Black soldiers who had enlisted alongside their neighbors. Women who traveled with the army and kept it running. Whoever Eddy is is yours to decide. Choose your race, gender, and background.

Before the first encounter: Take a moment. How did someone like you end up in this tent? What do you carry with you that the army doesn’t know about? Write one sentence, or one word, or nothing at all.

The Sight of It

(Heart, 0 Turns)

Your first roll. This one is free: it costs no turns, and every result leads to the same question. If you started at Steady, roll 2 dice and keep the higher. Then write your answer. The only way to lose here is to skip the moment, which costs 1 Heart wound.

You top the ridge and see it all at once.

A plateau above the Schuylkill, rimmed on three sides by hills, open to the south. The river bends below. In warmer months it might be something. It is not warmer months.

The column slows without anyone ordering it. Men stop mid-stride. The word used was camp. You had pictured a camp. You pictured tents, fires, a semblance of an organized thing. What you see is a cleared hillside, a handful of rough structures, and twelve thousand men who have been marching longer than anyone is counting.

The wind comes up the valley and finds you. This moment does not cost a turn. You only notice what you notice.

Heart is resolve. It is the place where connection and commitment meet. Pay attention to what it says because it is hardest to answer out loud.

The Last Mile

(Body, 1 Turn)

You’ve been marching since before dawn. The road from Whitemarsh was mud and then ice and then both. Your feet stopped reporting their temperature somewhere around midday. There is a tiredness that lives below thought. Your body is only moving out of habit.

Before anything else, you need to locate yourself in this body.

What are you carrying? The pack, the equipment, whatever belongs to your role in this army. What is it doing to you right now, at this exact moment, standing at the edge of this field?

Body is the physical instrument. Not what you think about your condition but what your body actually is. You’re reading it: what it can still do, and what it’s already spent.

7–12 Something specific is wrong: a blister, a shoulder, a knee. What is it? How long have you been ignoring it?

Reading the Camp

(Mind, 1 Turn)

You move through the arrivals: men sorting by regiment, officers directing traffic that doesn’t want to be directed, horses searching for water they can’t quite reach through the ice. You are watching without deciding to watch.

Your mind takes in the details and arranges them without being asked.

Here is what your mind sees: the officers are worried but not broken. The men who arrived two days before you have a specific look on their faces that you catalog without naming. Why You’re Here

(Heart, 1 Turn)

Your regiment has been pointed toward a section of hillside. That’s yours, apparently. You’ll build on it. That’s tomorrow’s problem. Tonight you’ll sleep on it, in whatever configuration keeps the wind off.

Someone asks you. The question people ask each other when they’re far from home and cold and nothing is what anyone expected. The words change but the question doesn’t:

Why are you still here?

Not why you enlisted. Not what you believe about the cause or the king or the rights of freeborn men. Why are you still here, in December, on this hill, with the road back to anywhere warmer still technically a road?

What do you say?

Roll Heart.

Roll Answer This In Your Journal
19–20 Heal 1 Heart wound. The answer comes without searching. You know exactly why you’re here. Write it down. Don’t soften it.
13–18 You have an answer. It has the right shape. Is it the true one? What would the true one sound like?
7–12 You say something. It sounds right enough. What did you say? Do you believe it?
1–6 Take 1 Heart wound. The pause is long enough to become its own answer. What is it that you can’t say out loud, even to yourself?

The Man from Your County

(Any Axis, 1 Turn)

You’re looking for where to put your kit when someone says your name. Not your rank. Not a general address. Your name from before.

He’s from two towns over. You know the face the way you know faces from a fair or a market day: vaguely, the way anyone knows anyone from the same county before the war made everyone’s county smaller. He looks as surprised as you do.

He has been here four days. He has opinions about it.

Choose your axis:

  • Body: He looks worse than you. You help him re-situate his shelter before the wind takes what’s left of it.
  • Mind: You trade information: which officers to know, where the wood detail goes, what the food situation actually is.
  • Heart: You talk. Really talk, the way people from the same place talk when they’re far from it.

Roll the chosen axis.

Roll Answer This In Your Journal
19–20 Heal 1 wound on the rolled axis. It goes better than you expected. You have someone here who knows your name from before. What does it feel like to hear it said?
13–18 It goes well enough, incomplete, useful in parts, better than nothing. What did you get from him that you needed?
7–12 Brief. He has somewhere to be. You’ll see him again, maybe. What didn’t you get to say?
1–6 Reroll the same axis once. On 7 or higher, use that row instead. On 1–6 again, take 1 wound on the rolled axis. The exchange didn’t land. What didn’t happen, and what did you lose?

Historical Notes

Continental Army soldiers arrived at Valley Forge on December 19, 1777, following a demoralizing fall campaign: the loss of Philadelphia in September, the defeat at Brandywine Creek, the failed counterattack at Germantown. The plateau was chosen for defensibility and proximity to Philadelphia (roughly twenty miles). The first night, many men slept outdoors in the snow. Washington had promised each company a reward for the first completed structure. Hut construction began immediately but the work would take most of December to complete in any meaningful way. Baron von Steuben would not arrive until February 1778.

Books & Primary Sources

  • Valley Forge — Bob Drury and Tom Clavin (Simon & Schuster, 2018). The most readable modern account of the encampment. Covers the soldiers’ daily experience in unusually human detail. Start here.

  • The Spirit of Seventy-Six: The Story of the American Revolution as Told by Participants — Henry Steele Commager and Richard B. Morris, eds. (Da Capo Press, 2002; orig. 1958). A massive anthology of firsthand letters, diaries, and accounts from the Revolution. Valley Forge is covered across multiple chapters. Excellent for a student who wants to hear the voices directly.

  • George Washington to the President of Congress, December 23, 1777 — Washington’s letter describing the army’s deteriorating condition and warning that it may not survive the winter. This is the primary source behind Encounter 1: https://www.loc.gov/resource/mgw3a.003/?sp=136&st=text

  • Women at Valley Forge — Women served the Continental Army at Valley Forge in documented roles: cooking, nursing, washing, and hauling water under fire. Their names appear in army records and pension files.

    • Hannah Till — An African American Quaker woman who cooked for Washington’s household at Valley Forge. Her name is recorded in Washington’s papers.
    • Margaret Thomas — Listed in camp rolls as a washerwoman. One of many women formally recognized in army records.
    • Mary Ludwig Hays — Later called “Molly Pitcher.” Present at multiple encampments and later at the Battle of Monmouth. Her service is documented in Pennsylvania pension records.
    • Valley Forge National Historical Park has compiled research on women at the camp. Begin here: https://www.nps.gov/vafo/learn/historyculture/valleyforgewomen.htm

Historical Notes

Within days of the army’s arrival at Valley Forge, Washington issued his order for hut construction on December 18, 1777. The specifications were precise: fourteen by sixteen feet, log walls, clay chinking, wooden roofs sealed against weather, fireplaces at the rear. Officers were housed separately from enlisted men, in better quarters. Washington himself reportedly stayed in a tent until enlisted men had huts, then moved to a stone farmhouse. This was a gesture noted by soldiers who wrote about it later.

The hut construction was a significant organizational accomplishment. Approximately 900 huts built in a matter of weeks under difficult conditions by men and women who were not carpenters, using tools that were often inadequate, working in December cold. The camp they built was functional, laid out in military order, and defensible. It was also cold, cramped, and underequipped for the winter they were about to survive.

Books & Primary Sources

  • Valley Forge — Drury and Clavin (see Encounter 1). The chapters on hut construction and the rhythm of camp life are among the book’s strongest.

  • Washington: A Life — Ron Chernow (Penguin, 2010). Pulitzer Prize winner. Covers Washington’s leadership under pressure in detail, including the organizational demands of sustaining the encampment.

Historical Notes

January and February 1778 were the worst months at Valley Forge. The camp subsisted on fire cake (flour and water baked over coals) and sometimes nothing at all. Washington estimated that on some days, the army had no provisions whatsoever. Desertion, which had been a persistent problem since the army’s arrival, reached its peak during this period. Washington’s letters to Congress describe the situation without softening: the army would starve, dissolve, or disperse unless something changed.

Albigence Waldo, a surgeon from Connecticut, kept a diary through the army’s first weeks at Valley Forge. It is one of the most immediate primary sources available on the experience of ordinary soldiers. His entries range from dark humor to despair to determination, often within the same day’s writing, and he kept treating patients with almost nothing to treat them with.

The death toll at Valley Forge (approximately 2,000) was from disease: typhus, typhoid, dysentery, and pneumonia. Enabled by malnutrition and exposure.

Historical Notes

Friedrich Wilhelm von Steuben arrived at Valley Forge on February 23, 1778. He began with a model company of 100 men, drilling them personally. He worked through his aide Pierre Stephen Duponceau, a young French-speaking American officer, shouting commands in German and French and having them translated as quickly as possible. Within weeks, the model company was being used to train other companies, and the drilling had spread through much of the army.

Von Steuben’s arrival coincided with the news of the French alliance. The Treaty of Alliance signed February 6, 1778, news of it reached the army in early May. The combination transformed morale. The army had survived the winter; France had entered the war; a Prussian general had personally drilled them into something. These three facts together produced the force that marched out in June. No single one of them was sufficient alone.

Historical Notes

The Continental Army left Valley Forge on June 19, 1778. They marched toward Monmouth, New Jersey, where the Battle of Monmouth on June 28 would be their first major engagement as the army von Steuben had trained. By most measures, the battle was inconclusive — but the Continental forces stood and fought the British in open field, something that had been genuinely uncertain before Valley Forge. The army that marched out was not the same force that had stumbled in.

Valley Forge was not an inevitable military crisis. It was the product of systemic failure. The Continental Congress had no reliable power to tax, the quartermaster system had nearly collapsed under Commissary General Thomas Mifflin, and the states were not meeting supply quotas. The soldiers who died at Valley Forge died of disease and exposure that adequate provisioning could have prevented. Washington’s enduring achievement at Valley Forge was not tactical. It was that he held the army together long enough for the system to partially correct itself. He did so through sheer presence, through letter-writing, and through refusing to leave.

Books & Primary Sources

  • Washington: A Life — Ron Chernow (see Encounter 2). Covers Washington’s growth as a commander across the Valley Forge winter and his recognition that this was the moment the army either became a real fighting force or died.

  • George Washington to Governor Patrick Henry, February 19, 1778 — Washington’s letter to the Governor of Virginia during the darkest months of the encampment, pressing for men and supplies.   • Regulations for the Order and Discipline of the Troops of the United States (“The Blue Book”) — Von Steuben (1779). See Encounter 4 for full citation and Library of Congress link. By Encounter 5, the Blue Book’s effect is visible.

  • Peter Muhlenberg — A Virginia pastor who accepted a commission in the Continental Army, eventually rising to brigadier general. He commanded troops through Valley Forge and fought at Brandywine, Germantown, and Monmouth. Primary source material is held at Virginia state archives and through the Library of Congress.

Closing The Adventure

June 19, 1778. Valley Forge, Pennsylvania. The tents are coming down. The huts are being left behind. The Schuylkill is below you. The road north is ahead.

The army that marched into this valley in December 1777 and the army that marches out today are not the same army. Von Steuben drilled twelve thousand men into something that moved and held together like soldiers. Three weeks from now, they will stand against British regulars at the Battle of Monmouth and not break. The war will go on for five more years. But something turned here, in the cold, before anyone was watching.

You survived the winter. That is not a small thing. Whatever wounds you carry out of this valley are real. Whatever you built, endured, or chose in these five encounters: it belongs to you now. The army is marching. You are marching with it.

Before you go, close the adventure.

Your Last Entries

Turn to a fresh page in your journal and title it Closing the Adventure. Work through the four steps below, then choose three questions from the Question Bank. Take your time. There are no wrong answers.

Look back. Page back through your journal and look at your tracker. Which axis carries the most wounds? Body, Mind, or Heart? Which encounter was hardest to write through? You don’t have to explain why. Just notice.

Eddy’s last entry. Write one final journal entry as Eddy Wake: the morning of June 19th, 1778, before the march begins. It can be a single sentence. It can be a list of things Eddy is leaving behind. It can be a question Eddy still doesn’t have an answer to. The only rule: it has to be true to who Eddy became during these five encounters, not who you thought Eddy would be at the start.

What you know now. Outside the journal (as yourself, not as Eddy) write down one thing you learned about Valley Forge that surprised you. One thing you didn’t expect. Not the most important thing. Just the one that caught you off guard.

The road ahead. Eddy is marching toward Monmouth, toward a long war, toward an outcome no one inside it could see clearly. Go deeper if you want to know what happened next: to the army, to von Steuben, to the women who kept the camp running, to the Black soldiers who fought without the promises they were owed.

Question Bank

The questions are grouped by what they’re reaching for. You don’t need one from each group, and you can answer more than three. Go where the energy is.

A. About Eddy’s Choices

  1. Which choice across the five encounters felt hardest to make? What made it hard?
  2. Was there a moment where you disagreed with what Eddy did, where you wanted to do something different but felt like you couldn’t, or chose not to? What held you back?
  3. Did Eddy end the week differently than they started it? What shifted?
  4. Was there a wound that felt deserved? Was there one that felt unfair?

B. About the History

  1. What surprised you most about what the real people around Eddy were dealing with?
  2. What did playing through this make you wonder about? What question do you still have?
  3. The adventure puts Eddy in the margins of history; present, but not recorded. Do you think there were real people like Eddy in this moment? What do you imagine their lives were like?
  4. Is there anything about this historical moment that reminds you of something happening now?

C. About the Journal

  1. Was there something you wanted to write about but couldn’t find the words for? What was it?
  2. Which encounter entry felt most honest to you? What were you actually thinking when you wrote it?
  3. Was there a moment in the story you didn’t write about in your journal but are thinking about now?

D. About Closure

  1. If Eddy could say one thing to the real people who lived through this moment what would it be? What would be the message to the ones history recorded and the ones it didn’t?
  2. What’s one thing you know now that you didn’t know when you began this adventure?

Part Three: Resources

Keeping Your Journal

If you’ve never kept a journal, here’s the short version: it is a record, in your own words, of what happened and what you thought about it. If it feels unfamiliar at first, that’s normal.

You are writing as Eddy, but you are still the one doing the thinking. If something surprised you, Eddy was surprised too. If a choice felt wrong but you made it anyway, write that down. The journal doesn’t have to sound like a history book. It should sound like someone who was there.

A few things that help:

  • Write in the past tense, as if you’re recording the day before sleep.
  • Be specific. “The soldier looked cold” is fine. “The soldier’s hands were shaking so badly he couldn’t hold the cup” is better.
  • If one question opens up into a whole scene, follow it. Write as much as you want.
  • If you made a choice you regret, write about that. The journal is private.

What a Strong Entry Looks Like

There is no right length. A strong entry does three things.

It is specific. It names what happened instead of summing it up. “I rolled Body at The Logging and got a 4. The axe handle split, I took a wound, and the others had to wait while I found another” is specific. “I did well today” is not.

It is honest. It says what Eddy actually felt, not what Eddy should have felt. “I think Eddy was relieved but also ashamed, and I’m not sure those two things can go together” is doing the real work.

It connects a choice to what followed. “I spent my last turn on Setting In instead of a second turn at The First Fire. I think that cost me something, but Eddy needed to sit with the day” shows a decision and owns it.

Outcomes in Axes RPG are not failures. An honest entry about a hard or uncertain outcome is exactly what the game is for.

What to Track

Your tracker holds your wounds. For each encounter, also note the axis and number of every roll you make. You will need those to work out your Tilt at the Encounter End.

Rules Reference

You won’t need this page often. It covers the rare cases.

Free healing. Some Encounter End results give you a free healing. Note it in your journal and use it any time: erase one wound on any axis.

Zero State. If every box on an axis is filled, that axis is in its Zero State:

Axis Zero State What it means
Body Collapse Your body cannot go on unaided.
Mind Breakdown You can’t think your way through what’s in front of you.
Heart Despair You lose the will to continue, and the thread to the people around you.

An axis in its Zero State can’t be rolled, and it can’t be healed by a table result. To recover, spend two turns in a row doing nothing but tending to it: no rolls. Then erase one wound. The Zero State is over. Your other two axes work normally the whole time.

Easier or harder starts. The standard start is Steady (fill both Peak boxes). For a gentler week, start at Peak with no boxes filled. This suits first-time players, younger students, or anyone for whom the story is heavy enough on its own. For a harder week, start at Worn by filling the Peak and Steady boxes. You will be playing from behind, and recovery becomes the story.

For the Learning Coach

This section is for the adult alongside a student. You don’t need to know the history deeply, and you don’t need to understand the rules. If your student asks a rules question you can’t answer, the answer is try it and see what happens. The game rewards decisions, not correct procedure.

Your Role

You are not the game master, and you are not the history teacher. The adventure handles the content. Your job is three things.

Make space. The student plays alone. Your job is a quiet place, the materials, and the understanding that this counts as school.

Ask one question. During the week, the only question worth asking is How is Eddy doing? It opens a conversation without steering it.

Listen. The journal is private. You can ask to read it, but don’t require it. Friday is where the student shares on their own terms.

The Friday Conversation

After Encounter 5 and the Closing, set aside 15–20 minutes. It’s a conversation, not a quiz.

  • Let them tell it. Ask them to walk you through Eddy’s week. Follow any thread worth following.
  • Don’t correct mid-thought. Let small historical errors go and come back to them later. Step in only for a mistake that distorts the whole discussion, such as who was involved or what caused what.
  • Follow up; don’t lead. “What made you think that?” is a good follow-up. “But don’t you think the deserters had a point?” is not, because it contains your answer.
  • Let silence breathe. A hard question may need ten seconds. Wait it out.
  • End with their words. Ask them to sum up the week in one sentence.

Five questions for Friday. Use any, in any order.

  1. Many soldiers left Valley Forge, and Eddy could have too. Why did Eddy stay? Was it the same reason in June as in December?
  2. About 2,000 people died at Valley Forge, nearly all from disease, and no battle was fought there. What caused the suffering? Could anyone have prevented it?
  3. Von Steuben said Americans needed to be told why before they would do something. Was there a moment when Eddy needed a “why”?
  4. Women, Black soldiers, and immigrants were all at Valley Forge, but they rarely appear in the paintings. Who did Eddy meet that history doesn’t remember? Why do some people get remembered and others don’t?
  5. Read Albigence Waldo’s diary entry aloud together (Encounter 3, Books & Primary Sources). How is it like your journal? How is it different?

You are not checking whether facts were retained. You are checking whether the student engaged with the experience, and whether Eddy’s story connected them to the history at all. Almost any honest answer is the right answer.

If You Get Stuck

Confused by the rules. Point them to How to Play. If that doesn’t help, simplify: pick an interaction, roll one die, and do what the table says. A session played with imperfect rules is still a session played.

Disengaged. Don’t push. Ask, “Is there something about Eddy’s situation that feels stuck?” Sometimes a student is sitting with something hard and isn’t ready to name it.

Rushed through without journaling. Don’t backtrack. The Closing gives them a chance to reflect on the whole week at once.

A history question you can’t answer. Say so, then look it up together or save it for Friday. The Books & Primary Sources after each encounter are a good place to start.

Wants to replay an encounter. Let them. There is no correct playthrough, and trying a different choice is exactly the kind of thinking the game is built for.

Using This in Your Curriculum

As a standalone week: the adventure is a complete five-day unit. No RPG experience is needed.

Alongside your spine: schedule it for the week your history spine reaches the winter of 1777–78. The Books & Primary Sources and First Stop lists after each encounter connect the story to further reading.
